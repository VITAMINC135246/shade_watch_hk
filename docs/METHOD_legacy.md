# Method, evidence, and departures

## Source and status

The principal research source is Wu et al., “Shade watch: Mapping citywide shade dynamics through ray tracing and LiDAR data in Hong Kong's complex 3-D built environment,” *Sustainable Cities and Society* 118 (2025), 106011, doi:10.1016/j.scs.2024.106011, supplied as `references/Shade Watch.pdf`. Sections 2.3–2.4 and Figures 3–5 describe the DSM, solar geometry, horizon scan, and 5° lookup. The authors' core software and supplementary Figure S1 were not supplied or verified. This repository is an independent reconstruction and has not run the authors' original software.

The supplied CEDD [2020 LiDAR metadata](<../data/raw/dsm/2020/Metedata for LiDAR Data (2020).html>) identifies the Hong Kong 1980 Grid System (EPSG:2326), HKPD vertical datum, and 20 December 2019–2 February 2020 helicopter acquisition. The GeoTIFFs themselves report 0.5 m square pixels and NoData = −9999. The supplied [terms](<../data/raw/dsm/Terms and Conditions of Use.pdf>) require attribution to the Hong Kong Government as source and owner. The 2020 DSM may differ from the 2026 scene.

## Geometry and shadow rule

- Convert the user-entered WGS84 location to EPSG:2326 and construct an exactly centred 600 m east–west × 450 m north–south, 1,200 × 900, north-up output grid (0.27 km²). The grid is shifted by a fraction of a native pixel relative to original tile edges; source elevations are mapped with nearest-neighbour sampling and are **not** described as finer than 0.5 m.
- Merge the source DSM over an initial 1,000 m buffer on every output side: 2.6 km east–west × 2.45 km north–south, or 5,200 × 4,900 cells. All available surrounding elevations can obstruct the central 1,200 × 900 pixels. Only central results are retained.
- Solar azimuth is clockwise from true north **toward** the Sun: 90° east, 180° south, 270° west. Obstruction rays travel toward the Sun; cast shadows point in the opposite direction. For a target surface height `z0` and a known sunward height `zj` at horizontal distance `d`, the obstruction slope is `(zj − z0)/d`. The horizon **elevation** is `atan(max(0, slopes))` above horizontal. The paper calls `90° − horizon elevation` the horizon **angle from zenith**. Solar zenith is `90° − apparent solar elevation`. Shade is assigned when solar elevation is strictly below the horizon elevation, equivalent to the paper's solar zenith exceeding the horizon angle.
- The baseline bins are 40°, 45°, …, 320°, with nearest 5° lookup. A needed direction outside that annual paper range is computed at its nearest 5° bin rather than silently clamped. The selected January day needs 115°–245°.

## Reconstructed horizon scan

`src/horizon.py` first rotates a narrow DSM strip into each sun-aligned direction, scans its columns using an upper-convex-hull tangent search, and maps horizons back to the original output grid. This follows the paper's rotation → one-dimensional sunward horizon → rotation-back sequence. The paper describes a Dozier-style recursive speed-up but omits precise resampling, edge, and tie-breaking settings. The upper-hull scan is our implementation choice, not a claim of code identity.

The common rotated 0.5 m strip can shift a target across a sharp canopy or roof edge. In the earlier 100 m run, this baseline disagreed with independently sampled original-grid rays at the 95th percentile by about 22° in horizon elevation, and its binary labels differed from the refined output for 4.47% of pixels. We therefore use **pixel-centred sun-aligned profiles sampled every 0.25 m** for the main GeoTIFFs, PNGs, JPG, MP4, and web viewer. Each profile uses original 0.5 m DSM values; the 0.25 m ray spacing is an integration step, **not** an upsampled DSM resolution. This is a documented numerical departure from the paper's raster-rotation implementation. The 5° LUT and nearest-direction lookup are retained. Directions are processed one at a time; the entire daily LUT and all frames are never resident in memory together.

Rays scan known elevations up to 1.5 km where the rectangular input extent provides them. **Complete support requires 1 km of valid DSM sunward of each target.** If an obstruction is found, shade can be proved even beyond a missing cell. If no blocker is found but the first kilometre has missing input, the pixel is invalid/uncertain rather than presumed sunlit. A separate conservative low-sun flag marks candidate sunlit pixels uncertain when a hypothetical obstruction as tall as the highest observed buffered DSM cell at 1 km could block them. The run inventory records its threshold; it is a sensitivity indicator, not a proof that terrain beyond the buffer is harmless at higher solar elevations.

The presentation JPG and MP4 show one native 0.5 m output cell per map pixel. Valid cells are rendered black for shade and white for direct sun; invalid or uncertain cells are gray. There is no nearest-neighbour enlargement or smoothing of the scientific labels. The earlier 100 m presentation enlarged a 200 × 200 result to 600 × 600 pixels, making each source cell a visible 3 × 3 block. Enlarging the study area adds surrounding buildings to the scene; it does not increase the DSM's native spatial resolution. The supplied SlimShady frame is a visual reference only; no SlimShady algorithm or data enter this reconstruction.

## Sun and time

`src/solar.py` uses the [NOAA fractional-year solar equations](https://gml.noaa.gov/grad/solcalc/solareqns.PDF) for equation of time, declination, hour angle, solar azimuth, and zenith. Apparent solar elevation applies [NOAA's piecewise approximate refraction correction](https://gml.noaa.gov/grad/solcalc/calcdetails.html). Sunrise and sunset use geometric solar-centre elevation −0.833° to approximate refraction plus solar-disk radius. This is a specified local implementation of solar position; the paper cites Szokolay (1996), but does not give enough detail to reproduce its exact software or refraction settings. All calculations use aware `Asia/Hong_Kong` local times and retain UTC timestamps.

The MP4 samples the chosen date's calculated **actual daylight** at clock-aligned 10-minute points. This differs intentionally from the paper's separate 06:00–18:00 annual analysis window. No nighttime interval is counted as building shade. Every MP4 image is a computed DSM result; no temporal image blending is used.

## Interpretation

The result is **clear-sky direct-sun obstruction at the DSM surface**. A tree pixel represents the canopy top, not illumination beneath it. A single-valued DSM cannot resolve overhangs or multiple vertically overlapping pedestrian surfaces. Clouds, atmospheric attenuation, reflectance, thermal comfort, and temperature are outside scope. No DTM is required for this core surface-obstruction calculation. Local CPU execution replaces the paper's Google Earth Engine delivery platform; its citywide runtime claims are not extrapolated to this computer.
